I have been using the **Ovonic X4** for a long time and still do not need another charger. I own plenty of batteries with different capacities and connectors, so having four channels in one unit is convenient. I bought it for my own use: this review reflects that experience, rather than a comparison of every current charger or a laboratory test.

More useful than simply saying I like it is explaining **when 500 W are enough and when you might hit a limit**. Four outputs do not mean that each one has the full power available.

## Why I chose the Ovonic X4

After using other chargers, I wanted a simpler setup. Several small chargers, an external power supply, cables and a case did not suit my preferences for convenience and budget.

The X4 combines four channels and mains power in one box. In my normal use, charging at 1C, I have not missed having more power. That does not mean it can charge any battery at any rate: the numbers matter.

Its colour display uses physical buttons rather than touch controls. That works for me. I have not missed an app or Bluetooth notifications either, although someone else may value those features more.

## The 500 W are shared

**A single output cannot deliver 500 W: its maximum is 250 W**, even with the other three outputs unused.

The manufacturer's specifications list the limits below. These are maximum power figures, rather than a guarantee that the selected current will be maintained throughout charging:

| Channels in use | Maximum power per channel |
|---|---:|
| One or two | 250 W |
| Three | Approximately 167 W |
| Four | 125 W |

There is also a **15 A limit per channel**. Both current and power limits apply. The three-channel figures are rounded; the advertised total remains 500 W.

## Example: a 5000 mAh 6S LiPo

First convert capacity to amp-hours: **5000 mAh = 5 Ah**. Charging at 1C means 5 A; at 2C it means 10 A, provided the battery manufacturer allows that rate.

A conventional 6S LiPo reaches **25.2 V** at full charge: six cells at 4.2 V each. Multiply voltage by current:

| Example rate | Current | Power at 25.2 V |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1C | 5 A | 126 W |
| 2C | 10 A | 252 W |

![Power calculation for a 5000 mAh 6S LiPo: 252 W at 2C]({{ '/img/reviews/ovonic-x4/calculo-6s.jpg' | relative_url }})

At 2C, 252 W slightly exceeds the 250 W maximum of one output. With four channels running, even the 126 W needed for the 1C example is slightly above the 125 W available per channel.

These calculations explain the limit near maximum voltage; **they are not measured charging times**. Current falls during the final charging stage, so doubling the rate does not necessarily halve the total time.

## Current can be a limitation too

Another infographic uses a **6700 mAh 4S battery at 3C** as a theoretical example:

- 6.7 Ah × 3 = **20.1 A** charging current.
- 4 × 4.2 V = **16.8 V** at full charge.
- 16.8 V × 20.1 A = approximately **337.7 W**.

That exceeds both limits of an output: 15 A and 250 W. The unit for current is **A, not Ah**; Ah measures capacity.

![Theoretical 6700 mAh 4S LiPo example at 3C, exceeding both current and power limits]({{ '/img/reviews/ovonic-x4/limites-4s-3c.jpg' | relative_url }})

This is not a recommendation to charge at 3C. Follow the battery manufacturer's permitted rate and conditions, select the correct chemistry and cell count, and supervise charging.

## Digital Power and connectors

One feature I like is **Digital Power**. It lets an output act as an adjustable power supply for an accessory, with voltage and current settings. Compatible workshop equipment, such as tyre warmers or a tyre truer, may benefit from it.

A matching connector alone does not establish compatibility. Check voltage, polarity and power requirements first. Also check which battery adapters you need: I have had to add to my own set, particularly for some bullet connectors.

## Who does it suit?

It suits me because I use many batteries, want four channels and prefer an integrated unit. If you charge large packs at high rates, or need more than 6S per battery, you will need a setup that meets those requirements.

My advice is to start with **your most demanding battery**. Calculate the charging current and power at maximum voltage, then account for how many packs you want to charge simultaneously. After that, consider connectors, power supply and convenience.

I made this mistake with an old iMAX B6 setup: I did not properly understand what my batteries required and ended up burning out the power supply. That is why I consider this calculation more useful than focusing on the biggest wattage number on the box.

**The X4 still does what I need.** The right purchase is the one that fits your batteries and charging habits, rather than necessarily the charger with the highest advertised power.
