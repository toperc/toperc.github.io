One of the channel's most frequent questions is **which battery to buy for the Wltoys 124017**. After using it off-road and in my asphalt project, my first check is simple: the pack must fit the car properly. Next I check the connector and whether its voltage suits the installed electronics.

<!--more-->

## Start with Shape and Dimensions

The video shows the long packs I use and how they are secured in the tray. Shorter, taller formats have caused clearance problems under the upper chassis.

**The mAh figure does not tell you whether a battery fits.** Compare length, width and height with the available space, including cable exits. The body must close and the restraint must hold the pack without crushing it.

The tray shown has two length positions. I use the longer setting in the recording; check your car before choosing a longer pack.

These observations concern the **124017**. I do not transfer the same battery choices to the 144001 or 144010, where available space differs.

## 2S and 3S: Confirm Compatibility First

I have used both voltages in my car. On 2S it is already fast; 3S requires much more care with steering and control, as the [full 124017 review]({{ '/reviews/wltoys-124017/' | relative_url }}) shows.

Compatibility depends on the electronics actually installed. **Check the ESC and the rest of the system before increasing voltage**: neither the model name nor a matching plug proves 3S compatibility.

## LiHV: Look at Fully Charged Voltage

The difference is not just nominal voltage. A conventional LiPo normally charges to **4.20V per cell**; a LiHV designed for it can reach **4.35V per cell**. For 3S, that means 12.60V versus 13.05V.

The [SkyRC charging documentation](https://www.skyrc.com/download/e450_Instruction_Manual_EN_V7.0.pdf) distinguishes these values. **The charger setting must match the exact battery**, and the car's electronics must accept its maximum voltage. Supporting conventional 3S LiPo does not by itself establish support for fully charged 3S LiHV.

## Capacity and Discharge: Do Not Just Chase the Biggest Number

The video uses packs around 2200mAh as a practical reference and explains why I would not chase huge C ratings for this setup. I also let the car cool before running another battery.

Consider actual discharge capability and system demand. A high label rating does not replace that check. More mAh does not force more current into the motor, though it can allow longer runs and greater heat accumulation.

My priorities are **compatible voltage, a suitable shape, the correct connector and a battery appropriate to the car's demand**. Then I consider capacity, weight and price. This avoids purchases that look perfect on paper but do not suit your actual car.
